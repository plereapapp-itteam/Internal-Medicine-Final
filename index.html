▪Hypertension

CategoryBP (mmHg)
Normal<120 and <80
Elevated120–129 and <80
Stage 1130–139 OR 80–89
Stage 2≥140 OR ≥90
  • Must know: Most hypertension is asymptomatic; severe disease may cause headache, visual symptoms, or dizziness.
  • DXHarrison assigns no single gold-standard test — diagnosis rests on accurate, repeated BP measurement.
  • RXFIRST-LINE: lifestyle modification plus a thiazide-type diuretic, ACE inhibitor/ARB, or calcium-channel blocker, chosen by comorbidity; combine agents if one does not control BP.
  • EMERAVOID ACEi/ARB in pregnancy or bilateral renal artery stenosis; AVOID beta blockers in uncontrolled asthma/advanced block; AVOID verapamil/diltiazem in advanced AV block or HFrEF.
  • DXHypertensive emergency is defined by acute cardiac, neurologic, or renal decompensation — a severe BP number alone is not sufficient.
  • RXFIRST-LINE IV options: nicardipine (start 5 mg/h, titrate by 2.5 mg/h, max 15 mg/h), labetalol (20 mg over 2 min, then 40–80 mg every 10 min, max total 300 mg), nitroprusside, nitroglycerin, or a condition-specific agent.
  • EMERAVOID abrupt normalization of BP in most hypertensive emergencies — Harrison recommends gradual MAP reduction over several hours unless the specific condition dictates otherwise.
  • Must know: Most-tested complications: stroke, coronary artery disease, heart failure.
Board trap: hypertensive urgency vs. emergency is decided by target-organ damage, not the BP number — severe asymptomatic elevation without organ injury is urgency (oral therapy, gradual control); acute end-organ decompensation is an emergency (monitored IV therapy).

▪Infective Endocarditis

Rapid-review targetBoard answer
Classic vignette openerFever + new/changing murmur ± embolic/immunologic manifestations
Classic findingPersistent bacteremia + valvular vegetation
Diagnostic criteriaModified Duke Criteria
Definite IE2 major OR 1 major + 3 minor OR 5 minor

  • DXLABMajor blood criterion: a typical organism recovered from 2 separate cultures, persistently compatible cultures, or a Coxiella burnetii criterion.
  • DXMajor echo/endocardial criterion: vegetation, abscess, prosthetic dehiscence, or new regurgitation.
  • Must know: Harrison calls TEE the optimal imaging modality; it does not name one single clinical "gold standard" separate from the Duke criteria/pathology.
  • RXFIRST-LINE: obtain blood cultures first, then start prolonged bactericidal IV antibiotics tailored to the organism.

Board trap: stable subacute IE vs. acute/unstable IE — in the stable subacute picture, wait for adequate blood cultures before antibiotics; in acute or hemodynamically unstable disease, draw 3 sets of cultures fast and start antibiotics without delay.

Rapid-review targetBoard answer

  • DXBiopsy is reserved for suspected giant-cell myocarditis or cardiac sarcoidosis.
  • RXFIRST-LINE: treat resulting ventricular dysfunction as heart failure.

▪Last-Minute "Do Not Pick This"

  • Must know: unstable tachyarrhythmia → do NOT "observe / obtain Holter first".
  • Must know: VF/pulseless VT → do NOT give medication before defibrillation.
  • Must know: cardiogenic shock with pulmonary edema → do NOT give large blind crystalloid boluses.
  • Must know: hypertensive emergency → do NOT normalize BP immediately.
  • Must know: STEMI with a diagnostic ECG → do NOT wait for troponin before reperfusion.
  • Must know: NSTEMI → do NOT give fibrinolytic therapy.
  • Must know: STEMI + recent sildenafil/vardenafil → do NOT give nitrate.
  • Must know: chronic HFrEF in pregnancy → do NOT use an ACE inhibitor/ARB.
  • Must know: hyperkalemia/advanced renal failure → do NOT uncritically initiate ACEi/ARB/MRA.
  • Must know: rheumatic MS + AF → do NOT choose a DOAC instead of warfarin, per the Harrison source.
  • Must know: acute pericarditis → do NOT give routine anticoagulation, given hemorrhagic-effusion risk.
  • Must know: tamponade with shock → do NOT delay pericardiocentesis for catheterization.
  • Must know: acute severe MR → do NOT choose outpatient observation.
  • Must know: severe symptomatic AS → do NOT choose "medical therapy alone" as definitive treatment.
  • Must know: chronic AR → do NOT choose a beta blocker as preferred AR-directed therapy, per Harrison.
  • Must know: TG >300 mg/dL → do NOT choose a bile-acid sequestrant.

▪Hepatic Encephalopathy and Hepatorenal Syndrome

  • Hepatic Encephalopathy
  • DXClassic opener: confusion or personality change after GI bleed, infection, constipation, sedatives, dehydration, or electrolyte disorder in a cirrhotic patient.
  • DXClassic finding is asterixis; there is no single numeric diagnostic criterion — this is a clinical diagnosis made after identifying the precipitant and excluding alternatives.
  • RXFirst-line drug is lactulose, titrated to an endpoint of 2–3 soft stools/day; rifaximin is the preferred alternative/add-on.
  • RXAvoid sedatives — especially benzodiazepines when avoidable — and avoid treating a serum ammonia number rather than the patient.
  • EMERSevere obtundation requires airway protection plus correction of the precipitating disorder; the most-tested complication is aspiration/coma.
  • Hepatorenal Syndrome
  • DXClassic opener: advanced cirrhosis/refractory ascites with a progressive creatinine rise ↑ without clear structural renal disease — functional renal failure in advanced portal-hypertensive liver disease.
  • LABNo complete formal numeric criteria are given; it is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out hypovolemia, nephrotoxins, infection, obstruction, and intrinsic renal disease.
  • RXFirst-line source therapy is IV albumin plus midodrine plus octreotide; liver transplantation is the definitive treatment.
  • RXAvoid NSAIDs, nephrotoxins, and unnecessary hypotensive therapy.
  • EMERProgressive renal failure with circulatory decompensation requires hospitalization and transplant evaluation; the most-tested outcome is death from progressive liver/renal failure.
Board trap: Serum ammonia level assumed to track encephalopathy severity — ammonia does not reliably correlate with clinical severity, so treat the patient's mental status and precipitant, not the number.
Board trap: Rising creatinine in cirrhosis labeled hepatorenal syndrome too early — HRS is a diagnosis of exclusion only after hypovolemia, nephrotoxins, infection, obstruction, and intrinsic renal disease are ruled out.

  • DXClassic vignette opener: hypercalcemia with nephrolithiasis, osteoporosis, constipation, weakness, or neuropsychiatric symptoms ("stones, bones, groans, psychiatric overtones").
  • LABClassic biochemical finding: hypercalcemia ↑ + inappropriately elevated/nonsuppressed PTH. Reference calcium in Harrison's figure: 8.9–10.1 mg/dL.
  • LABAlbumin correction: add 0.8 mg/dL to measured calcium per 1 g/dL that albumin is below 4.1 g/dL.
  • DXKey mimic — familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH): PTH is often nonsuppressed, but urinary calcium is low (distinguishing test).
  • RXFIRST-LINE definitive when indicated: parathyroidectomy. Surgery indications: age <50, nephrolithiasis, CrCl <60 mL/min, T-score <−2.5, or serum Ca >1 mg/dL above normal.
  • EMEREmergency: severe symptomatic hypercalcemia → saline-based hypercalcemia treatment. Most-tested complication: nephrolithiasis.
Board trap: never operate for FHH — parathyroid surgery is contraindicated in familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia; the low urinary calcium (not the PTH level) is what separates it from primary hyperparathyroidism.
Severe hypercalcemia / hypercalcemic crisis
  • DXClassic vignette opener: cancer or hyperparathyroidism + dehydration + confusion + constipation/polyuria. Severe cutoff: Ca >13 mg/dL.
  • EMEREmergency clue: very high calcium levels can progress to coma/cardiac arrest.
  • First diagnostic discriminator: PTH level → separates PTH-dependent from PTH-independent hypercalcemia.
  • RXFIRST-LINE emergency therapy: IV normal saline hydration. Preferred sustained therapy: IV bisphosphonate (pamidronate/zoledronate).
  • RXRapid ALT/add-on: calcitonin — rapid onset, but tachyphylaxis develops after 1–2 days. Special rescue: dialysis in extreme circumstances/renal failure.
  • EMERMost-tested complication: coma / cardiac arrest.
Board trap: AVOID forced diuresis before adequate volume repletion — give IV normal saline first; diuretics before rehydration worsen volume depletion.

▪Hypoparathyroidism / Hypocalcemia

  • DXClassic vignette opener: after thyroid/neck surgery → perioral tingling + hand spasm/tetany. Classic findings: Chvostek sign + Trousseau sign + prolonged QT.
  • LABBiochemical pattern: calcium ↓ + phosphate ↑ + PTH low/inappropriately normal.
  • LABImportant check: measure magnesium — severe hypomagnesemia can prevent calcium correction. Gold-standard concept: serum/ionized Ca + PTH + Mg/phosphate together define the mechanism.
  • RXFIRST-LINE chronic: calcium 1–3 g/day + calcitriol 0.25–1 µg/day.
  • EMERLIFE-SAVING symptomatic: calcium gluconate 1–2 g IV over 10–20 min. Preferred add-on: correct any magnesium deficiency.
  • EMERMost-tested complication: tetany / laryngospasm or seizure.
Board trap: repeated calcium alone fails if severe hypomagnesemia persists — magnesium must be corrected for calcium to normalize; do not keep re-dosing calcium without checking Mg.
  • Hypoparathyroidism = Ca ↓, phosphate ↑, PTH low/inappropriate; primary hyperparathyroidism = Ca ↑, PTH inappropriately high/nonsuppressed.

▪Psychiatry Rapid-Fire Board Traps

  • ≥5 depressive symptoms × ≥2 weeks = MDD; depression + prior decreased sleep/grandiosity = bipolar disorder, not unipolar MDD.
  • Hallucinations/delusions ≥6 months with no medical cause = schizophrenia; new psychosis + fluctuating attention = delirium — find the medical cause.

  • Little sleep + grandiosity + pressured speech = mania, treated with a mood stabilizer, never antidepressant monotherapy.
  • Lithium patient + thiazide/NSAID + tremor/confusion = lithium toxicity, not a routine dose increase.
  • Intrusive contamination fear + repetitive washing = OCD, treated with SSRI + ERP, not benzodiazepine monotherapy.
  • Antipsychotic + rigidity + fever + CK ↑ = NMS — stop the antipsychotic; do not continue or rechallenge it.
  • Suicidal plan + intent + accessible means = hospital-level safety, not routine outpatient reassurance.

▪Post-Stroke Spasticity, Immobility, Falls & VTE

Spasticity
  • Must know: Classic scenario is a flexed/pronated arm or an equinovarus leg posture weeks to months after stroke, with increasing resistance to rapid passive movement. SUPPLEMENTARY Formally defined as a velocity-dependent increase in resistance to passive stretch, commonly graded with the Modified Ashworth Scale, though tone score alone should not drive treatment.
  • RXFIRST-LINE for all spasticity is positioning, ROM/stretching, task-specific movement, and treating aggravating triggers. SUPPLEMENTARY Focal disabling spasticity FIRST-LINE is botulinum toxin A; generalized spasticity ALT is oral baclofen or tizanidine; severe refractory generalized spasticity ALT is intrathecal baclofen.
  • EMERSudden new spasticity with neurologic deterioration should prompt evaluation for recurrent stroke or acute illness, not just more antispastic therapy. Most-tested complication is fixed contracture.
Immobility, falls, VTE, pressure injury
  • Must know: Classic scenario is a hemiparetic survivor who is mostly bedbound with poor balance and cannot transfer independently; no single test applies — assess gait, transfers, balance, skin, cognition, and device safety.
  • RXFIRST-LINE is progressive mobility with transfer/gait/balance training, repositioning, and pressure protection; an appropriate cane/walker/wheelchair/orthosis is chosen by functional assessment. DVT prophylaxis is an explicit step in the Harrison stroke algorithm.
  • EMERNew unilateral leg swelling or acute dyspnea/hypoxemia↑ in a bedbound stroke patient must be urgently evaluated for DVT/PE; most-tested complication is falls/fracture or DVT/PE.
Board trap: continuing routine gait therapy vs stopping for a red flag — unsupervised walking in a patient with severe neglect, poor balance, or unsafe cognition, and continuing rehab through a suspected DVT/PE, are both AVOID answers.
  • Focal disabling spasticity = botulinum toxin A first-line; generalized spasticity = oral baclofen/tizanidine, escalating to intrathecal baclofen if severe and refractory.
